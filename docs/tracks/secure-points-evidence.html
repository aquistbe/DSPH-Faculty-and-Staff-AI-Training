The Evidence Behind Secure-Point Assessment

Companion to Assessment That Holds Up to AI · Teaching & Learning track · ~20 min read

← Back to Assessment That Holds Up to AI

What the research shows for each of the five patterns, what 2026 sources add, and where the evidence runs out.

Summary

Checking understanding at a few secure points, and letting AI be used elsewhere, follows current guidance from regulators and universities, and research supports it. No study has evaluated this combination of patterns as a whole, and none measures misconduct as an outcome. The evidence is strongest for oral assessment and for not relying on AI detectors. It is indirect for checkable values and planted-error tasks.

Pattern What makes it a check Strength of evidence Key sources

Oral checkpoints

Oral assessment has the strongest evidence of any pattern, but nearly all of it comes from live, interactive orals with follow-up questions. Recorded orals without follow-up have much less direct evidence.

Evidence that oral assessment works

  • Huxham, Campbell & Westwood (2012) randomly assigned 99 biology students to oral or written versions of the same questions. Students scored significantly higher in the oral version, and no group of students was disadvantaged. Students were more nervous about orals but many found them more useful, and they described them as more “professional.”
  • Joughin (1998) sets out six dimensions of oral assessment, including how much interaction there is between student and examiner. It is a useful framework for deciding what kind of oral you’re running.
  • Akimov & Malin (2020) describe oral exams in an online course and conclude they can help with problems of online assessment, including academic dishonesty, if they are planned for validity, reliability, and fairness.
  • Pearce & Chiavaroli (2023), writing in Medical Education, argue for reclaiming oral assessment where unassisted understanding matters, and embracing AI for other assessment.
  • Newell (2025) describes interactive orals in psychology as a response to ChatGPT, because students must show their knowledge in real-time conversation.

Documented implementations

  • Griffith University. Sotiriadou et al. (2020) evaluated scaffolded assessments ending in interactive orals, using a student survey. Results suggested the format helped prevent misconduct and built professional and communication skills. Griffith has since published 20 examples of different interactive oral designs.
  • Dublin City University has used interactive orals since 2020. Ward et al. (2023) give four case studies (computing, education, French literature, aviation). A 2025 report funded by Ireland’s quality agency surveyed 322 students (42% response) in disciplines including law, psychology, and nursing, and seven academics. Both groups named academic integrity as a benefit. The report gives no misconduct data.
  • University of Sydney. Its two-lane model lists viva voces and interactive orals among typical secure assessments.

Recorded orals. Pentland et al. (2026) report the first two course studies of asynchronous oral assessments, delivered on a web platform:

  • In an accounting course, the link between taking part in the orals and exam performance was positive but not statistically significant.
  • In a data analytics course, students scored higher on the orals than on in-person multiple-choice exams, and the two correlated moderately.
  • Students prepared differently, used more active study strategies, and found the orals professionally relevant.

The orals ran alongside in-person exams, as a complement, not as the only secure measure.

Gaps. The studies above measure marks and student or staff views. None measures misconduct rates. Orals resist AI mainly because examiners can ask follow-up questions; a recorded oral has none, so a student could read a prepared script. A question about the student’s own submission is the strongest protection available in that format, but no study has tested it.

A value you can check

The evidence here is indirect. No study evaluates a “report the value you retrieved” task as an integrity measure. The reasoning rests on evidence that language models produce specific, plausible, wrong details.

  • Walters & Wilder (2023) analyzed 636 citations in ChatGPT-written literature reviews on 42 topics. 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Of the real ones, 43% and 24% had substantive errors.
  • Chelli et al. (2024) asked models to reproduce the references of published systematic reviews. Hallucination rates were 39.6% (GPT-3.5), 28.6% (GPT-4), and 91.4% (Bard).
  • Linardon et al. (2025) tested GPT-4o in June 2025. 19.9% of 176 citations were fabricated, and 45.4% of the real ones had errors. Fabrication was 6% for depression but 28–29% for less-studied disorders. Errors rise as topics get narrower, which is why a specific local value is harder to fake.

Citations are the closest tested stand-in for a retrieved data value: a specific item you can check against its source quickly. No comparable study tests models retrieving local public health estimates.

Why a checkable value fits the theory. Corbin, Dawson & Liu (2025) distinguish rules students can ignore from structural changes to the task. Checking a value against its source is structural. Dawson et al. (2024) argue that validity (whether the task shows what the student can do) matters more than cheating as such. Retrieving and correctly reading an estimate with its uncertainty is a competence worth certifying.

Limits. All of these error rates come from models without live web access, and they are falling. Tools that browse can retrieve real values, so this pattern is weakening as a check. It keeps its value as a skills task, especially when an oral asks the student to explain the value.

Planted-error critique

The case for learning is reasonably strong. The case for AI resistance is weaker and depends on how the errors are built.

Evidence that finding errors supports learning

  • Adams et al. (2014) assigned 208 middle-school students to find, explain, and fix errors in worked decimal problems, or to solve the same problems. There was no difference on the immediate test. On a delayed test, the error-finding group did better.
  • Große & Renkl (2007) found that adding incorrect solutions to worked examples helped mainly students who already knew more, though a later study did not replicate that pattern. Tsovaltzi et al. (2012) found benefits mainly when students got support in detecting and correcting errors. Implication: run a critique task after students have the concepts, and give them guidance on what to look for.
  • Bearman et al. (2024) argue that higher education must now develop students’ evaluative judgement of AI outputs and AI processes. A planted-error critique practices this directly.

Documented implementations

  • Lerret (2026), in medical laboratory science, had students use AI to draft case studies and then correct errors in analytes, reference ranges, and diagnostic reasoning, with a paired video. 15 of 17 responding students preferred it to the traditional assignment.
  • Pence et al. (2024) and Ruff et al. (2024), in chemistry, had students evaluate AI output. Students found inaccuracies, including in calculations and in methods.
  • These are single-course reports with small samples and student-perception outcomes. None measures integrity.

Grading against a key has not been tested against open-ended critique. The reasoning stands on its own: an open count rewards volume, AI produces plausible critiques on demand, and a key makes false findings cost points.

Build log graded on the quality of checking

The evidence supports the pattern’s own premise: a written account of process proves nothing about who did the work. It also supports grading the log for what it teaches.

Process writing is easy to generate

  • Li et al. (2023) had experienced pharmacy teaching staff grade ChatGPT-written and student-written reflections with a rubric. The ChatGPT reflections scored higher on all six criteria. A trained classifier told them apart far better than the staff did.
  • Scarfe et al. (2024) submitted AI-written answers to real take-home psychology exams at a UK university. 94% went undetected, and on average they scored half a grade boundary above real students.
  • Kofinas et al. (2025) ran marking experiments at two UK universities. Markers generally could not tell work with AI input from work without, and making a task more “authentic” did not help. The authors recommend more performative assessment, and less reliance on asynchronous written work.

Grading the checking

  • Tai et al. (2018) define evaluative judgement as the capability to decide on the quality of one’s own and others’ work, and argue it should be developed deliberately.
  • TEQSA’s 2023 propositions include assessing “the process of learning.” Its 2026 report says process evidence should sit “alongside assessment products,” not replace them, and should not be seen as “a foolproof method of assuring learning.”
  • Winstone, Gravett & Elkington (2026) propose “Black Box Assessment”: assess process, treat errors and revisions as evidence of progress, and reward the learning path, not just the polished product. That supports giving credit for documented corrections in a log.

Audit of dated claims

The skill has good evidence, including from an asynchronous online course. Its value as security is weak.

  • Wineburg & McGrew (2019) compared professional fact checkers, historians, and Stanford undergraduates evaluating websites. Fact checkers read laterally: they left a site quickly and opened new tabs to see what others said about it. They reached better-supported conclusions in a fraction of the time.
  • McGrew et al. (2018) found that students from middle school through college struggled to evaluate online claims, sources, and evidence.
  • Breakstone et al. (2021) embedded lateral-reading modules in an asynchronous online college nutrition course. Students improved significantly from pretest to posttest. It is the closest documented setting to an online public health course.

No published account describes students auditing their own instructor’s dated lectures. It is a new combination, and it is best treated as practice for the later tasks.

Choices across the course

Choice Evidence Strength
Check at a few points, not everywhere TEQSA’s 2023 propositions (“security at meaningful points across a program”) and 2025 follow-up (at least one secure task per course, as a hurdle); Sydney’s two-lane model Strong consensus; policy and argument, not trials
Reduce workload and time pressure Abbas et al. (2024): a three-wave survey of 494 students. Higher workload and time pressure predicted more ChatGPT use One longitudinal study; observational
No AI detectors Weber-Wulff et al. (2023): 14 tools, including Turnitin, “neither accurate nor reliable.” Perkins et al. (2024): simple edits cut accuracy by 17.4%. Liang et al. (2023): seven detectors flagged 61.3% of TOEFL essays as AI-written on average Strong on unreliability; the bias finding is disputed (below)
State the AI-use level on every assignment The AI Assessment Scale is a widely used model. Corbin et al. (2025) argue such scales are rules, not controls, and create an “enforcement illusion” Useful for clarity; not a control

Two findings worth knowing

  • A ban alone does not hold. A 2026 preprint (not yet peer reviewed) analyzed about 7,500 applications to a US public policy master’s program. In 2025, most applicants submitted at least one essay that was mainly AI-written, despite an explicit ban. This fits Corbin et al.: a “no AI” rule is enforced only by whatever check sits behind it.
  • The detector bias finding is disputed. The same preprint found no difference in false-positive rates for international applicants or TOEFL and IELTS takers, using a commercial detector with a low false-positive rate. The authors note that detectors tuned to avoid false positives may miss more AI writing. The case against detectors rests on unreliability and easy evasion, not on bias.

What 2026 sources add

2026 guidance supports the direction of secure points, oral and process-focused assessment, and not relying on detection. It also exposes a gap: in a fully asynchronous course, the only secure measure is unsupervised and remote.

Source Type What it recommends or finds
Crompton & Burke (2026), IJETHE Systematic review of 72 empirical studies, 2024–2026 Three main design responses to generative AI: process orientation, authenticity, and oral or dialogue-based approaches
Pentland et al. (2026), ETR&D Two course studies Recorded orals scored higher than in-person multiple-choice exams and correlated moderately with them; they ran alongside those exams
TEQSA: Lodge, de Barba et al. (2026) Regulator-commissioned report Continue to use secure assessment “until more sophisticated approaches emerge”; process evidence alongside products; process data is not “foolproof”
Winstone, Gravett & Elkington (2026), AEHE Conceptual model Assess how students build their work; value errors and revisions; call for structural, not just rule-based, responses
QAA, The Perfect Storm (2026) UK quality agency report Inconsistent AI rules across modules and staff confuse students and staff. Involve students in AI policy, prioritize consistency, and train staff and students
Villena, HEPI (2026) Policy commentary “Detection is a losing strategy.” Require a minimum share of assessment that is “demonstrably non-delegable,” using orals, iterative projects, supervised problem-solving, and portfolios

The counterpoint. A 2026 Policy Exchange report by Phil Newton, Evidence of Learning Through Assessment, calls on UK universities to end unsupervised remote exams “immediately” and return to in-person assessment, according to Times Higher Education’s coverage. The coverage also notes the report points to wider use of the interactive oral viva in much of Europe. We have read the coverage, not the report itself.

Put plainly: a fully asynchronous course cannot meet that bar. Recorded orals are unsupervised and remote. The defense of secure points in an asynchronous course is narrower. Students must explain their own work on a timer, at several points, and the first study of recorded orals is broadly favorable. That is not a claim that recorded orals are secure in the supervised sense. Where you can, add a live or supervised element, or offer one as an option.

Three other 2026 papers look relevant but are not summarized here, because their full text has not been read: Weidlich’s Which inference is at risk?, Nicola-Richmond et al. on a program-wide redesign, and Dao & Hwang on a streamlined interactive oral model.

Limits of the evidence

  • No study measures misconduct as an outcome. Oral assessment studies report marks and student or staff views. The one randomized comparison (Huxham et al., 2012) predates generative AI.
  • The oral evidence is mostly for live orals. Recorded orals without follow-up questions have one recent pair of studies, and there they complemented in-person exams.
  • AI error rates are a moving target. Every hallucination figure above comes from models without live web access, now one to three generations old. Tools that browse do better.
  • Several patterns have no published implementations. None was found for auditing an instructor’s dated lectures, for retrieved-value tasks framed as a check, or for key-graded planted-error critiques.
  • The case rests on converging indirect evidence plus strong expert consensus. If you use these patterns, collect your own data. For example, compare oral checkpoint performance with written grades for each student, and track how often reported values match the source.
