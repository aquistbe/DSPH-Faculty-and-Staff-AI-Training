Core 1 · Foundations & Capabilities

Core 1 · Foundations & Capabilities

What generative AI is, which Drexel tools to use, and how to prompt for useful results

One hour | Bring a laptop | Runs weekly in the fall term and repeats in spring — dates announced by school email

📊 Open the slides ↗ ⬇ Editable PowerPoint

What You’ll Leave With

No experience needed. If you have never opened ChatGPT or Copilot, this session is for you. By the end of this hour, you will be able to:

  1. Say in one sentence what tools like ChatGPT do, and what they get wrong
  2. Know which Drexel tools to use and what information never goes into them
  3. Sign in to ChatGPT Edu, type a request, and ask a follow-up
  4. Use four simple questions (PTCF) to get a better answer

NoteA Few Words You’ll Hear
  • Prompt: what you type into the box. A question, a request, or instructions.
  • Chat: one conversation. The tool remembers what was said earlier in the same chat, but not in other chats.
  • Model: the AI system underneath the tool. You can use the default.
  • Hallucination: when the AI states something false as if it were true.

What AI Is, and Which Tools to Use (8 min)

What it is. ChatGPT and Copilot write text by predicting what words should come next, based on patterns in a huge amount of writing. That makes them good at drafting, summarizing, rewriting, and explaining. It also means they can sound confident and still be wrong. They are not search engines or databases.

Which tools to use. For Drexel work, use ChatGPT Edu or Copilot Chat, both signed in with your Drexel account. Other tools, including personal ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, are for public or low-risk information only. Full details are on the Tools & Access page.

ImportantThe One Data Rule

Never paste in health information about a person, student records, Social Security numbers, or identifiable research data. When in doubt, leave it out or make the details up.

TipThe Mental Model

Think of AI as a fast, eager assistant who has read a lot but is sometimes wrong. You tell it what you need, it writes a draft, and you check the draft before you use it.

How AI Gets Things Wrong, and Who Is Responsible (7 min)

It makes things up. Because it predicts likely-sounding text, it can invent facts, numbers, and citations. Check anything that matters before you use it.

It can be biased. It learned from writing that reflects society’s inequities, so its answers can repeat stereotypes. In public health, that matters: our work affects real communities. (For a well-known health-care example, see the Bias & Equity Audit track.)

You are responsible. If your name is on it, you own it: accuracy, fairness, and tone. “The AI wrote it” is never the answer. We return to these rules in Core 4: Verify, Disclose, Decide.

Guided Tour: ChatGPT Edu, Step by Step

Guided Tour: ChatGPT Edu, Step by Step

Duration: ~10 minutes | Format: Follow along with the instructor

Goal: Everyone signed in and comfortable with the screen before we try real tasks.

Can’t sign in to ChatGPT Edu? Open Copilot Chat with your Drexel account instead, or pair with a neighbor. Everything today works in either tool.

  1. Sign in. Go to ChatGPT Edu and sign in with your Drexel account. Check that you are in the Drexel workspace (the name appears near the top or bottom left).

  2. Find the message box. It is at the bottom of the screen. This is where you type your prompt.

  3. Type a test prompt and press Enter:

    In two sentences, what can you help a public health staff member with?

  4. Ask a follow-up in the same chat:

    Give me three examples.

    Notice that it remembered your first question.

  5. Copy the answer. Hover over the response and click the copy icon, then paste it anywhere.

  6. Start a new chat. Click “New chat.” The new chat does not remember the old one.

  7. Find your history. Your past chats are listed on the left. You can rename or delete them there.

Activity 1: Your First Three Requests

Activity 1: Your First Three Requests

Duration: ~15 minutes | Format: Individual, hands-on

Goal: See what the tool does well by giving it everyday tasks.

Copy each prompt below into ChatGPT Edu, one at a time. Read what comes back. There are no wrong answers here.

Request 1 — Summarize

Summarize this in 3 bullet points for a busy colleague:

Between 2018 and 2022, pedestrian deaths in the United States rose 18 percent, the highest level since 1981. Most of these deaths happened in cities, often on wide, fast roads without sidewalks or crosswalks. Black and Hispanic pedestrians and adults over 65 were killed at higher rates. Many cities have Vision Zero plans, but most focus on education campaigns instead of redesigning streets, which the evidence shows works better.

Request 2 — Draft an email

Write a short, friendly email reminding my committee that their report sections are due Friday at noon. Keep it under 100 words.

Request 3 — Explain

Explain what a “data use agreement” is in plain language, in about 100 words, for someone new to public health research.

Then try a follow-up. Pick the answer you liked most and type one of these: “Make it shorter.” “Make it more formal.” “Add a sentence about ___.”

Quick Reflection

  • Which answer was most useful as-is?
  • What did you need to change or add that the AI couldn’t have known?

Activity 2: Better Requests with PTCF

Activity 2: Better Requests with PTCF

Duration: ~15 minutes | Format: Hands-on, then share with a neighbor

Goal: See how a little more detail gets you a much better answer.

Four Questions to Ask Before You Type

Letter Question Example
Persona Who should it act as? “You are an experienced department administrator.”
Task What do you want it to do? “Write an email announcing a staff meeting.”
Context What does it need to know? “The meeting is Tuesday at 10 in Room 204. We will discuss the new travel policy.”
Format What should the answer look like? “Under 100 words, warm tone, date and time in the first line.”

You don’t need all four every time. Adding even one or two makes a difference.

Try It

  1. Type this short prompt and look at the answer:

    Write an email about a meeting.

  2. Now start a new chat and type the full version:

    You are an experienced department administrator. Write an email announcing a staff meeting. The meeting is Tuesday at 10 in Room 204, and we will discuss the new travel policy. Keep it under 100 words, use a warm tone, and put the date and time in the first line.

  3. Compare the two answers. Which one could you send with the fewest changes?

  4. Your turn. Think of a real email or short document you need to write this week. Write a prompt for it using the four questions. Use made-up names and details if needed.

Share

Show a neighbor your prompt and the answer. Suggest one detail they could add.

NoteSame Prompt, Different Answer

If you run the same prompt twice, you may get slightly different answers. That is normal. When a prompt works well, save it in a document so you can reuse it.

Wrap-Up (5 min)

  • AI writes drafts. You check them and decide.
  • For Drexel work, use ChatGPT Edu or Copilot and follow the data rule.
  • Before you type, ask the four questions: Persona, Task, Context, Format.

Try this before Core 2: pick one task you do every week—an email, a summary, a set of notes—and do it once with ChatGPT Edu using PTCF. Bring it to the next session.
